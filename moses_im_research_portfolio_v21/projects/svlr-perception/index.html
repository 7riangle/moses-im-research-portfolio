When does vowel length change what we hear?

A jsPsych-based speech-perception task developed from Ferragne et al.’s (2011) brood–brewed paradigm, with an extended two-context design and a short working demonstration.

01 / Background

The question behind the experiment.

In Scottish English, vowel length depends partly on a word’s phonological and morphological structure. The Scottish Vowel Length Rule (SVLR) means that the vowel in brood is typically shorter than in brewed (Aitken, 1981; Scobbie et al., 1999). But do listeners experience that difference as two distinct categories, or as predictable variation?

This project began from Ferragne et al. (2011), who investigated how listeners identify brood versus brewed along a vowel-duration continuum. I adapted that methodological starting point for a web-based experiment and introduced a comparison between two sentence contexts.

Designed and implemented as a University of Edinburgh coursework experiment. This is an experimental demonstration, not a published or validated finding.

02 / Experimental design

Building on earlier work.

Following Ferragne et al. (2011), I proposed a seven-step vowel-duration continuum (approximately 50–185 ms) based on Praat PSOLA resynthesis. This was a planned stimulus manipulation, not a fully produced and validated seven-step audio set. The coursework implementation used my own recording as a placeholder to demonstrate the identification task.

My adaptation puts the target word in a fixed carrier sentence rather than in isolation, adds a second sentence-context block, and collects a 1–7 confidence judgement instead of Ferragne et al.’s category-goodness rating.

03 / Implementation

Adapted and implemented in jsPsych.

I developed the browser-based implementation with JavaScript and jsPsych: preloading, trial randomisation, audio playback, F/J or mouse responses, response time, and confidence judgements. The online procedure also adapts the anti-phase headphone screening method described by Woods et al. (2017).

Stimulus limitation. Ferragne et al. used a Glasgow speaker’s recording. In my coursework implementation I used my own recording as placeholder audio. The proposed seven-duration continuum was not completed as a validated stimulus set, so this demonstration cannot test a psychometric identification curve or provide evidence about Scottish listeners’ categorisation.

  1. Preload the representative placeholder audio files for the two contexts (along with the audio and headphone checks).
  2. Present a fixation for 500 ms, then play the stimulus.
  3. Collect a forced-choice judgement: F for BROOD, J for BREWED (or on-screen buttons).
  4. Ask for confidence on a 1–7 slider, then advance to the next randomised stimulus.

Planned coursework protocol: 20 repetitions per proposed step and context (7 × 20 × 2 = 280 main trials). The live demo is intentionally much smaller and does not instantiate this full design. The single context frame may itself favour BREWED, so this block is exploratory rather than a controlled test of morphological conditioning.

04 / Portfolio adaptation

Original sequence. A very short demonstration.

The public demo keeps the original sequence—consent, volume calibration, Woods et al.’s six-trial headphone check (with the original pass threshold), practice, and two blocks. It is shortened to one practice trial per context and two main trials per context, using representative placeholder audio rather than pretending to present a seven-step acoustic continuum.

Planned protocol

No response data are saved or transmitted. This is a non-research portfolio demo. PHP saving is not deployed. This demonstration preserves the task sequence, not the planned continuum manipulation or the full research protocol.

05 / Sources

Methodological foundations.

This project extends an existing perceptual paradigm rather than claiming to invent the method. These are the principal sources behind its design:

  • Ferragne, E., Bedoin, N., Boulenger, V., & Pellegrino, F. (2011). The perception of a derived contrast in Scottish English. Proceedings of ICPhS XVII, 667–670. Identification continuum and original perception task.
  • Aitken, A. J. (1981). The Scottish Vowel-length Rule. In So meny people, longages and tonges, 131–157. Linguistic background for SVLR.
  • Scobbie, J. M., Hewlett, N., & Turk, A. E. (1999). Standard English in Edinburgh and Glasgow: The Scottish Vowel Length Rule revealed. In Urban Voices, 230–245. Speaker differences and conditioning of vowel duration.
  • Wightman, C. W., Shattuck-Hufnagel, S., Ostendorf, M., & Price, P. J. (1992). Segmental durations in the vicinity of prosodic phrase boundaries. JASA, 91(3), 1707–1717. Motivation for avoiding a phrase-final target.
  • Woods, K. J. P., Siegel, M. H., Traer, J., & McDermott, J. H. (2017). Headphone screening to facilitate web-based auditory experiments. Attention, Perception, & Psychophysics, 79(7), 2064–2072. Anti-phase headphone check.

Headphone-screening stimuli and source. The six anti-phase screening files used in this demonstration match the stimulus set distributed with the McDermott Lab HeadphoneCheck repository (GitHub), associated with Woods et al. (2017). The screening method and sounds are not my original work. The repository is distributed under the BSD 2-Clause license. The downloadable project includes its license notice. Exact provenance of the locally supplied audio files should be confirmed before publication.
